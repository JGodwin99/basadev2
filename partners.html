Our Approach

How BDI raises money through partnerships.

Partners are fundamentally different from institutional donors. While donors provide grants, partners provide co-investment - contributing their own resources, networks, and capabilities alongside BDI's. This distinction shapes how BDI approaches partnership development: not as a funding transaction, but as a strategic alliance where both parties gain.

BDI's partnership strategy targets a 10 new formal MOU partnerships per year through 2029. Each MOU establishes the basis for resource sharing, joint programming, or consortium funding arrangements. The goal is not volume - it is the right partners in the right areas, with the depth of relationship needed to unlock the next level of BDI's impact.

Critically, partners can also function as a pathway to institutional funding. When BDI partners with a larger INGO or a national institution like NaFAA, that partner often serves as the primary contract holder for grants that BDI could not independently access - channeling resources through to BDI as a co-implementing partner.

Consortium Co-Implementation

BDI joins a larger INGO or national institution as a co-implementing partner on grants they lead. BDI brings local presence, community trust, and field capacity; the partner brings the grant contract and fiduciary management. Both organizations share in the delivery and the credit.

Sub-Granting and Resource Channeling

Larger partners sub-grant a portion of their institutional funding to BDI to implement specific program components within our geographic or technical areas of strength. This is currently how BDI's relationship with Conservation International (GEF) operated.

Private Sector Co-Investment

Private sector partners — from agribusinesses to technology companies to Liberian enterprises — co-invest in BDI programs that advance their sustainability or CSR objectives. In return, BDI provides program design, community access, and impact reporting aligned with the partner's goals.

In-Kind and Technical Partnership

Not all partnerships are financial. Some partners contribute expertise, equipment, training materials, venues, or staff time — resources that reduce BDI's operational costs and free up grant funding for direct program delivery. These contributions are valued and tracked as part of BDI's total resource mobilization.

Partnership Funding Share

From 20% today to 25% of a much bigger budget by 2029.

Since BDI was formally established in 2022, approximately 20% of all resources mobilized have come through partner channels — primarily through co-implementation with Conservation International on the GEF SBCRP project, and through the in-kind and technical contributions of national CSO and network partners.

By 2029, BDI projects partnership funding to grow to 25% of our $2 million annual target — equivalent to $500,000 per year. This growth reflects both an expanding portfolio of formal MOUs and a deliberate strategy to use partnerships as a bridge to institutional funding pools BDI cannot yet access independently.

Why are partners growing faster than institutions? As BDI's credibility increases, more organizations will seek to partner with us — not just fund us. The 5-percentage-point growth in the partner share (20% to 25%) reflects BDI becoming a sought-after co-implementer in the Liberian development ecosystem, not just a grant recipient.

What Partners Contribute to BDI's Mission

Six ways partners make BDI more powerful than it could ever be alone.

The value of partnerships at BDI goes far beyond co-funding. Partners are the mechanism through which BDI accesses expertise it does not have, communities it has not reached, and funding it could not unlock on its own.

Financial Leverage

Every $1 BDI receives through a consortium partner can unlock $3-5 in grant funding that BDI could not independently access. Partners serve as financial multipliers - turning BDI's local credibility into access to much larger funding pools.

Technical Expertise

Partners bring specialized technical knowledge that deepens BDI's programs: biodiversity conservation expertise from CI, fisheries management knowledge from NaFAA, digital rights literacy from WACSI. This expertise improves program quality without increasing BDI's overhead.

Geographic Reach

Some partners have established community relationships and physical presence in counties where BDI is still building its network. Partnering with these organizations allows BDI to extend its program reach into new areas faster and with greater community trust than going in alone.

Credibility and Endorsement

When a well-known INGO, government body, or respected CSO network publicly partners with BDI, it signals to other donors and communities that BDI is a trusted, capable organization. Partnership portfolios are themselves a form of institutional credibility that BDI actively builds.

Capacity Building

Many of BDI's partners contribute to BDI's organizational development — providing training, mentorship, systems support, and professional development opportunities for BDI staff. This investment in BDI's people and systems is as valuable as any financial contribution.

Advocacy Amplification

Network partners like NCSCL and DARA amplify BDI's policy voice in national conversations around digital rights, civil society law, and community development policy. BDI's advocacy reach is multiplied many times over through the collective voice of its partner networks.

Partnership Tiers

Not all partnerships are equal — and BDI treats them accordingly.

BDI structures its partnership relationships across three tiers, each with different levels of engagement, resource commitment, and strategic priority. This structure ensures BDI invests its partnership management capacity where it generates the most value.
